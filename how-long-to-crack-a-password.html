How Long Does It Take to Crack a Password?

Published November 21, 2026 · 6 min read · By KeyGuard Pro

The honest answer is "it depends on the password and on how the site stored it", but you can still get useful numbers. This guide gives a crack-time chart by password length and character type, shows how the figures are calculated, and explains why they are best read as a comparison between passwords and not as a prediction.

Crack time by password length

The chart assumes a password made of randomly chosen characters. It also assumes the worst case for the defender: an attacker who has stolen a database of fast-hashed passwords and can test 10 billion guesses per second. Times are the average, which is the time to search half of all possible combinations.

How the numbers are calculated

For a random password, the number of possible combinations is the size of the character set raised to the power of the length. A 12-character password of letters and digits has 6212, about 3.2 × 1021, combinations. On average an attacker finds it after trying half of them, so the average time is that half divided by the guessing speed:

average time = (character set sizelength ÷ 2) ÷ guesses per second

At 10 billion guesses per second, that is about 1.6 × 1011 seconds, or roughly 5,100 years. The same arithmetic produces every cell in the chart. For the bits-based view of the same idea, see our guide to password entropy.

What the chart shows

  • Length beats variety. Going from 12 to 14 characters of letters and digits multiplies the time by about 3,800. Switching a 12-character password from letters and digits to all symbols multiplies it by about 150.
  • Short passwords fall quickly whatever they contain. An 8-character password is cracked in days at best, even with every symbol on the keyboard.
  • Digits only is weak at any realistic length. PINs and numeric codes are only safe because services limit how many guesses you can make.

Why real crack times differ from the chart

The site's hashing makes a huge difference

Sites should store a slow, salted hash of your password, not the password itself. Slow hashing such as bcrypt, scrypt or Argon2 can cut an attacker's speed from billions of guesses per second to thousands or fewer per graphics card. The chart uses the worst case, a fast hash, so against a well-run site your real margin is far larger. You cannot see which kind of site you are dealing with, which is why it is safer to plan for the worse case.

Human-chosen passwords are much weaker than the chart

The chart is only valid for passwords produced by a random generator. A password like "Summer2026!" is not a random 11-character string. Attackers try common words, names, dates and predictable patterns first, so it would fall in a tiny fraction of the time the chart suggests for its length. This is covered in more detail in how passwords get cracked.

Online guessing is far slower than offline cracking

The 10 billion guesses per second figure applies only after a database leak, when the attacker can work on their own hardware. Against a live login page, rate limits and lockouts hold guessing to a tiny fraction of that speed. A leaked password reused on other sites is a bigger practical risk than raw guessing.

Hardware keeps improving

Guessing speeds rise over time. A password that looks safe by a wide margin today keeps its margin longer if you choose extra length now. Adding two characters is cheap and multiplies the work by thousands.

How long should your password be?

  1. Use at least 14 to 16 random characters for important accounts such as email, banking and your password manager.
  2. If you want something you can type from memory, use a passphrase of six or more randomly chosen words.
  3. Use a different password everywhere. The crack time of a strong password is irrelevant if it leaked from another site.
  4. Store them in a password manager and turn on two-factor authentication, which protects you even when a password is guessed or stolen.
