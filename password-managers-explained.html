Password Managers Explained: What to Look For

Published November 8, 2026 · 7 min read · By KeyGuard Pro

A password manager is a program that creates, stores and fills in your passwords so you only have to remember one. It is the practical answer to the question "how do I use a different strong password on every site?" This guide explains how they work, what to look for, and how to avoid the most common setup mistakes.

How a password manager works

A manager keeps your logins in an encrypted vault. You unlock the vault with a master password, and often a second factor, and the software then fills in credentials for you. Most managers also include a generator, similar to the one on this site, so each new account gets a long random password that you never need to see.

In well-designed products the vault is encrypted on your device before it is stored or synced. The company holds only encrypted data and cannot read your passwords. This is often called a zero-knowledge design. It is also why a forgotten master password may be unrecoverable.

Why they improve security

  • They make unique passwords practical. Reuse is the most common weakness, and managers remove the effort that causes it.
  • They help against phishing. Autofill matches the website address. If the page is a lookalike, the manager will not offer your login, which is a useful warning.
  • They allow long, random passwords without any memorizing.
  • They can flag weak, reused or exposed passwords so you know what to fix first.

Types of password manager

  • Standalone apps and extensions from specialist companies, usually with syncing across devices.
  • Browser and operating system managers built into your browser, phone or computer. They are convenient and have improved a lot, but features and cross-platform support vary.
  • Local-file managers that store the vault as a file you sync yourself. They give you more control and more responsibility.

No single type is right for everyone. A built-in manager that you will actually use is far better than a sophisticated one you avoid.

What to look for

  1. Strong encryption done on your device, with a clearly documented security design.
  2. Independent security audits published by the vendor.
  3. A good track record, including how the company responded to any past incidents.
  4. Two-factor authentication support for the vault itself.
  5. Syncing across the devices you use, and encrypted export so you can leave.
  6. A clear emergency or recovery option, such as an emergency contact or recovery key.

Setting one up safely

Choose a strong master password

This is the one password that protects everything else. Use a long random passphrase of seven or eight words, as described in our guide to passphrases, and use it nowhere else.

Turn on two-factor authentication for the vault

See our guide to two-factor authentication. A hardware key or an authenticator app is best.

Save the recovery information

Print the recovery key, if you get one, and keep it somewhere physically safe. Without it, a forgotten master password may mean losing the vault.

Move accounts over gradually

Start with your email, banking and work accounts. Each time you log in somewhere, let the manager save the login and then replace the old password with a generated one.

Common concerns

"Isn't it risky to put everything in one place?" It is a trade-off, but for most people reusing weak passwords is a much larger risk than a well-protected vault. A strong master password plus two-factor authentication makes the vault a hard target.

"What if the company is hacked?" With a zero-knowledge design, attackers who steal vault data get encrypted files that are still protected by your master password. A long random master passphrase makes that data practically useless.

"What if I lose my phone?" The vault is synced, so you can sign in on a new device with your master password and second factor, which is why backup codes matter.

Habits that keep it working

  • Lock the vault when you step away from your computer.
  • Keep the app and browser extension updated.
  • Review the security report occasionally and fix reused or weak passwords.
  • Never type your master password into a link you clicked in an email.
Put it into practice

Generate a strong random password in your browser. Nothing is sent to a server.
