Password Security Guides

Practical guides to choosing, storing and protecting passwords. Each one explains the reasoning rather than just listing rules, so you can apply it with or without our tools.

Why password advice is worth reading properly

Most people learn about passwords from a login screen that asks for a capital letter, a number and a symbol, and from advice that has been repeated for twenty years. Some of that advice still holds. Some of it, such as changing every password every ninety days or relying on substitutions like "P@ssw0rd", has been dropped by the organisations that once recommended it, because it produces passwords that are hard for people and easy for software.

What has replaced it is simpler to explain and harder to argue with. Length and unpredictability do most of the work. Reuse is the failure that turns one breach into many. A password manager makes long random passwords practical, and two-factor authentication protects an account even when the password is known. These guides take each of those ideas in turn, show where the numbers come from, and describe the cases where the usual advice does not fit.

How to use these guides

If you are setting up a new account, read the entropy guide and then use the password generator. If you are trying to remember a password you must type, the passphrase guide and the passphrase generator will be more useful. If an account of yours has been in the news, start with the breach checklist. If you are choosing a password manager, read that guide before you compare products, so you know which features matter and which are marketing.

Each guide ends with related reading, so you can follow a topic further without coming back to this page. The FAQ answers the short questions that come up most often, and the breach check lets you test whether a password has appeared in known leaked data.

How these guides are written

The guides are written for KeyGuard Pro and checked against public sources rather than assembled from other articles. The main references are NIST Special Publication 800-63B, which sets out current thinking on password length and complexity for US federal systems, the Electronic Frontier Foundation's documentation of its 7,776-word list for passphrases, and the published documentation for the Have I Been Pwned Pwned Passwords service. Where a number appears, such as the 12.9 bits added by one random word from that list, it can be worked out from those sources.

Advice changes. The date at the foot of each guide shows when it was last revised, and corrections are welcome through the contact page. These guides are general guidance for ordinary accounts; they are not a security audit, and they do not cover every threat, such as a compromised device or a targeted attack on one person.

What the guides do not cover

They do not recommend specific paid products, they do not provide legal or financial advice, and they do not promise that following them makes an account impossible to break. Security depends on the website holding the account, the device you use and the people who can reach it, as well as on the password itself. What the guides can do is remove the most common and most avoidable weaknesses.
