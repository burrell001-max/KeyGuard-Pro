How to Create a Strong Master Password

Published November 26, 2026 · 7 min read · By Kent Williams

The master password is the one password in your life that you have to remember yourself. Everything else can be filled in by a password manager. Choose this one badly and every account behind it is exposed; choose it well and you rarely have to think about it again.

What the master password protects

A password manager stores your logins in an encrypted vault. The master password is the key that unlocks that vault, and the manager derives the encryption key from it each time you sign in. The manager does not keep the master password anywhere, and it cannot recover it. That is the point: if someone obtains the encrypted vault without the master password, they have a file they cannot read.

This creates two opposite risks that you have to balance. A master password that is easy to remember may also be easy to guess, and a breach of the vault would then expose everything. A master password that is impossible to remember gets written on a sticky note beside the screen, which is worse. The aim is a password that is strong against guessing but still memorable to you after a few days of use.

Why it must not be reused

If your master password is also the password for your email or a shopping site, a breach at that site puts the vault at risk. Attackers routinely take leaked email-and-password pairs and try them against other services. A master password has to be unique to the vault, and it should not be a small variation on a password you use elsewhere.

The same logic applies to the vault's own account. Treat the master password as belonging to exactly one thing. If you have already used a similar password somewhere, change it now rather than later, and check whether it has appeared in leaked data using the breach check.

Length first, then memorability

Random words are the practical answer. A passphrase of six words chosen at random from the EFF's 7,776-word list carries about 77 bits of entropy, which is comparable to a 12-character random password made of letters and digits, and far easier to type from memory. Seven words take it to roughly 90 bits. Our passphrase generator uses that list and picks each word with your device's cryptographic random number source.

What matters is that the words are random, not that they form a sentence you invented. A phrase from a song, a quote or a personal story follows patterns that cracking tools try early. If you choose the words yourself, you are relying on an attacker not knowing you, which is not a plan. Generate the phrase, then learn it.

Learning a passphrase you did not choose

A generated phrase looks strange at first. Type it slowly a few times, then say it aloud in chunks. Most people find six words manageable within a day or two, especially when they type it each morning to unlock the vault. Do not shorten it because it feels awkward on the first attempt; the awkwardness fades and the length is the protection.

If your manager supports a longer phrase, or a PIN plus a device-bound key, use what fits your life. What you should avoid is a short password with clever substitutions, such as a word with letters replaced by numbers, which satisfies complexity rules while remaining predictable.

Things not to build it from

  • Names of family members, pets or places you talk about publicly.
  • Dates, phone numbers, postcodes or anything visible on a profile.
  • Words from the site's own name, or the phrase "master password" itself.
  • A password you already use, with a digit changed at the end.
  • Anything you have written in a message, a note app or a document that syncs to a shared account.

Writing it down, carefully

Even a memorable phrase deserves a backup, because memory fails after an illness, a long break or a house move. Writing the phrase on paper and storing it somewhere physically secure is a reasonable compromise for many households, provided the paper is not kept beside the device and does not say what it is for. Others prefer to split it: half in a safe, half with a trusted person. Whatever you choose, the goal is that losing the master password does not mean losing every account at once.

A recovery kit should also include the vault's recovery codes, the email address tied to the account and the name of the manager you use. Keep it offline. Do not email it to yourself, and do not store it in a cloud note that is protected only by the account you are trying to protect.

Changing it, and what to do if it leaks

There is no need to rotate a master password on a schedule. Change it when there is a reason: you suspect it was seen, you typed it on a device you do not trust, or you are replacing a phrase you have outgrown. Most managers can change the master password without re-encrypting every entry by hand, but check the manager's instructions first, and make sure you have a current backup before you start.

If the master password is exposed, treat every account in the vault as exposed. Change the master password, then work through the important accounts in order: email first, then banking and any account that can reset the others. The data breach guide sets out that sequence, and the two-factor guide explains how to add a second layer that survives a lost password.

Questions people ask

Is a six-word passphrase really enough?

Six randomly chosen words from the 7,776-word list give about 77 bits of entropy. That is strong for an offline attack when the vault uses a modern, slow key-derivation function, which reputable managers do. Seven words give more margin at the cost of a little more typing.

Can I let the browser remember it?

You should not rely on a browser's saved-password feature as the only copy of a master password. It stores the secret in a place that may sync to an account of its own, and it can be read by anyone who gets into that browser profile.

What if I forget it?

Without the master password or a recovery method the manager provides, the vault cannot be decrypted. That is why the recovery kit matters. Write down the phrase and the recovery codes, store them offline, and test that you can still read them after a few months.
