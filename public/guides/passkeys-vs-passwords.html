Passkeys vs Passwords

Published November 26, 2026 · 7 min read · By KeyGuard Pro

A passkey is a way of signing in without sending a secret to the website. More sites offer them every year, and where they are available they remove several of the weaknesses that make passwords such a problem. They are not a full replacement yet, though, and it helps to understand what they protect against before you switch.

What a passkey actually is

A passkey is a pair of cryptographic keys: one private key that stays on your device, and one public key that the website stores. When you create a passkey, your device generates the pair and sends only the public half to the site. That public key is useless on its own. At sign-in, the site sends a challenge, your device signs it with the private key after checking that it is talking to the right website, and the site verifies the signature with the public key it already holds.

Because the private key never leaves your device, there is no shared secret sitting in a company's database for an attacker to steal. The kind of breach that exposes millions of passwords, and the credential stuffing that follows it, has nothing to work with. The underlying standards are WebAuthn, published by the W3C and FIDO Alliance, and the FIDO2 project that defines the authenticator side.

How signing in with one works

  1. You enter your username on the site, as usual.
  2. The site asks your browser for a passkey. Your device shows a prompt, often asking for a fingerprint, face or device PIN.
  3. Your device checks that the site's address matches the one registered with the passkey, then signs the challenge.
  4. The site verifies the signature against the public key stored for your account and signs you in.

Nothing you know is typed into the page. The browser talks to the device's authenticator directly, whether that is a phone, a laptop with a fingerprint reader, or a separate security key. The prompt you see is generated by your operating system, not by the website, which is part of why a fake page struggles to imitate it.

Why they resist phishing

A password can be typed into any page that looks convincing. A passkey is bound to the website's domain when it is created, and the device refuses to use it for a different domain. If you are tricked into visiting a lookalike address, the browser will not offer the passkey for the real site, and the attacker cannot ask for it. This is the single biggest advantage over passwords and one-time codes, both of which can be captured by a well-made fake login page and reused.

Passkeys also remove the need for one-time codes sent by text message, which can be intercepted or SIM-swapped. Where a site supports passkeys, they are generally a stronger second factor or replacement than an SMS code, and much less effort at sign-in.

Where the passkey lives, and what happens if you lose it

Most passkeys today are synced by a platform keychain, such as iCloud Keychain, Google Password Manager or a password manager that supports the standard. Syncing means a new phone can sign in without re-registering every site, and it means losing a device is not automatically losing the accounts. Some passkeys are device-bound instead, stored on a single security key that you carry. Both approaches are legitimate, and which one you have depends on the site and the authenticator you chose.

Recovery deserves a plan. If a passkey is only on one device and that device is lost, broken or wiped, you need another way into the account. Many sites let you register more than one passkey, which is the simplest protection: add your phone and a second device or security key. Check what recovery options the site offers, keep the account's email address accessible, and do not remove your last passkey until a replacement is registered and tested.

Where passwords still matter

Passkeys are not universal. Plenty of sites still rely on passwords, some passkey implementations fall back to a password when you sign in on a new device, and shared or public computers may not be suitable for a passkey held in your personal keychain. There are also accounts where you cannot use a password manager at all, such as the device login that unlocks the keychain itself.

That is why the advice on this site has not changed: use a unique, long, random password everywhere a password is required, keep those passwords in a manager, and turn on a second factor. A passkey is a better option when a site offers it, not a reason to stop caring about the passwords that remain. Should you switch now?

If a service you use offers passkeys, adding one is a small improvement with little downside, provided you keep a working recovery route and do not delete your password until you are confident. Start with accounts that matter most and are attacked most: email, banking and any account that can reset the others. Leave the rest for later. The transition will take years, and a good password plus two-factor authentication is still a reasonable place to be while it happens.

Questions people ask

Can a passkey be stolen in a data breach?

A breach of a website can expose the public keys it stores, but those cannot be used to sign in. The private key stays on your device or in your keychain. An attacker who has your unlocked device is a different problem, which is why device locks and screen locks still matter.

Do passkeys work across brands?

Increasingly, yes. The standard allows passkeys to move between platforms, and some password managers can store them regardless of device brand. Support varies by site and by the software you use, so check before you rely on it.

Are passkeys more private?

They reduce what a site has to store, since it never holds a password. That does not make the site anonymous: it still knows your account, and it may still use cookies and analytics as any website does. On this site, the Privacy Policy and Cookie Policy cover that side.
